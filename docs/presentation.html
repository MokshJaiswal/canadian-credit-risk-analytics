Analogy

Interest is rent on money. Rent an apartment, pay rent. Rent $500,000 from a bank, pay interest. The rate is the rent price.

  1. A loan is money a bank gives you now, which you promise to pay back later, plus a fee.
  2. The amount you borrow is the principal. For example, five hundred thousand dollars.
  3. The fee is interest. It's charged as a percentage of what you owe, every year. That percentage is the interest rate.
  4. What you still owe at any moment is the balance.
  5. Here's the easiest way to think about it. Interest is rent on money. You rent an apartment and pay rent for using it. You rent money from a bank and pay interest for using it. The interest rate is the rent price.

  1. Most loans are repaid in equal monthly payments. Each payment has two parts.
  2. Take a five hundred thousand dollar loan at one point nine percent, paid over twenty-five years. The monthly payment is two thousand and ninety-five dollars.
  3. In the first month, seven hundred and ninety-two dollars of that is interest. That's the rent.

The gap

A 25-year mortgage is five separate 5-year rate deals in a row.

  1. A mortgage is a loan to buy a home. The home is the collateral. If you stop paying, the bank can sell it.
  2. Every Canadian mortgage runs on two different clocks. The first is the amortization: how long until the loan is fully paid off. Usually twenty-five years.
  3. The second is the term: how long the interest rate is locked. Usually five years.
  4. When a term ends, you renew. You sign a new rate, at whatever rates are at that time. You can't keep the old one.
  5. So a twenty-five year mortgage is really five separate five-year rate deals in a row. The gap between those two clocks is the entire project.

  3. A variable rate moves with the bank's prime rate, every month. When rates rise, the pain arrives gradually.

  1. Where do interest rates come from?
  2. It starts with the Bank of Canada, the country's central bank. It sets the policy rate, which is the base price of money in Canada. It raises it to fight inflation and cuts it to support the economy.
  3. Banks set their prime rate from it. In this data, prime is always the policy rate plus two point two percentage points.
  4. Variable mortgages and home equity lines of credit are priced off prime.
  5. Fixed mortgages follow something else: the five-year Government of Canada bond yield. That's the interest investors earn when they lend money to the government for five years.
  6. On top of their base rate, banks add a spread, to cover their costs, their risk, and their profit.

  1. Now, a trap that a previous version of this project fell into.
  2. The Bank of Canada publishes a five-year mortgage rate. But it's the posted rate. A sticker price that almost nobody pays.
  3. In January 2021, the posted rate was four point seven nine percent. But the five-year bond yield was zero point four three percent, and people were actually paying around one point eight percent.

A pawn shop keeps your item if you don't pay. An IOU from a friend leaves them with nothing. That's why the IOU costs more.

When borrowers struggle

The journey of a late loan

Hazard

Monthly chance a current loan misses a payment. About 2 in 1,000 for mortgages.

Cure

A late borrower catches up.

Roll

A late borrower misses again and moves one box right.

  1. What happens when someone can't pay?
  2. Each month, every loan is in exactly one state. Most of the time, it's current. Paid on time.
  3. If the borrower misses a payment, the loan becomes thirty days past due. The monthly chance of that happening is called the hazard. It's small. About two in a thousand for mortgages.
  4. From there, two things can happen each month. The borrower can catch up. That's called a cure.
  5. Or they can miss again, and roll forward. Sixty days. Ninety days. A hundred and twenty days.
  6. Ninety days or more is called impaired. The bank now seriously doubts it will be repaid.
  7. After a hundred and twenty days, the loan goes into default. The bank stops expecting normal repayment, and the loan leaves the book.
  8. Delinquent simply means behind on payments. Anything from thirty to a hundred and twenty days.

Preparing for losses

The bank's rainy-day fund

Provision

You don't know which day your car breaks down, but you know roughly what repairs cost each year, so you save that much. A provision is the bank's rainy-day fund for bad loans.

Expected loss = Balance × PD × LGD

Balance (exposure)

How much is owed.

PD: probability of default

Chance they stop paying in the next 12 months.

LGD: loss given default

Share really lost: 10% mortgage (house is sold) · 85% card (nothing to sell).

  1. A bank knows some loans will go bad. It can't know which ones, but it can estimate how much. And it's required to set that money aside in advance.
  2. That money is called a provision. Think of it as a rainy-day fund. You don't know which day your car will break down, but you know roughly what repairs cost each year, so you save that much.
  3. The size of the fund comes from one formula. Expected loss equals the balance, times the probability of default, times the loss given default.
  4. The balance is how much is owed. Bankers also call it exposure.
  5. The probability of default is the chance the borrower stops paying. We use the chance over the next twelve months.
  6. The loss given default is the share of the balance that is really lost if they do default. For a mortgage, the bank can sell the house, so we assume only ten percent is lost. For a credit card there is nothing to sell, so it's eighty-five percent.

Analogy · a slippery road

Most days everyone drives safely. Weaker drivers skid more. Ice (higher rates) makes everyone skid more. After a skid you recover (cure) or slide further (roll).

  1. The second notebook plays out forty-two months, from April 2023 to September 2026.
  2. Each month, every current loan has a small chance of missing a payment. That chance, the hazard, is built from three parts.
  3. A base rate for the product. These are set so each product lands near published Canadian default rates over this period: about half a percent for mortgages, three percent for auto loans, five percent for cards.
  4. A credit score effect. Every hundred points lower doubles the chance.
  5. A real rate effect. Every percentage point the loan's rate has risen since signing adds twenty-five percent.
  6. A where factor, from real arrears data. Saskatchewan's mortgages fall behind two point five eight times as often as Canada's, so its borrowers carry that much more risk. Quebec's carry less.
  7. And a when factor, from real unemployment. As unemployment rose from 2023 to 2025, risk rose everywhere. Why two factors? Real data showed unemployment explains when arrears rise, but not where they're highest.
  8. Think of it as driving on a slippery road. Most days everyone drives safely. A weaker driver skids more often. Ice on the road, which here means higher rates, makes everyone skid more. And once you skid, you either recover or slide further.

The approach · a design rule

Store amounts, not ratios

ProvinceLate $Total $Rate
A$1$1010%
B$1$1,0000.1%
Average of the two rates5.05% ✗
Sum ÷ sum$2$1,0100.2% ✓

Illustrative numbers. Tables store delinquent_balance in dollars; every rate is SUM ÷ SUM.

  1. One design rule matters a lot in banking dashboards: store amounts, never ratios.
  2. Imagine two provinces. Province A has one dollar late out of ten. That's ten percent. Province B has one dollar late out of a thousand. That's zero point one percent.
  3. If you average the two rates, you get about five percent. That's wrong. It treats a ten-dollar book the same as a thousand-dollar book.
  4. The right way is to add up the late dollars and the total dollars first, then divide. Two dollars out of one thousand and ten. About zero point two percent.
  5. So the tables store the late balance in dollars, and every rate is calculated as a sum divided by a sum. These are illustrative numbers, but the trap is real and very common.

Challenges

Bugs found by asking "does this make sense?"

Shared random seed

Month 1: mortgages 93 late (≈30 expected), cards zero. Same seed reused the product-picking numbers. Fix: separate seed.

Data leakage

"More months → less default": backwards. Defaulted loans leave the data. AUC 0.84 was fake; honest is 0.76.

Non-reproducible model

Rows came back in a different order each run, changing the split. Fix: sort by loan id.

Wrong regional driver

Unemployment by province ranked Atlantic Canada riskiest. Real arrears say Saskatchewan. Fix: real arrears for where, unemployment for when.

Lesson

A result that runs without errors can still be wrong. Trace one example back to its source.

  1. Other problems were found by asking one question about every result: does this number make sense?
  2. In the first month of the simulation, mortgages had ninety-three late loans when about thirty were expected, and credit cards had none at all. The cause: both notebooks used the same seed, so the random numbers that decided who missed a payment were the same ones that had decided each loan's product. The fix was a separate seed.
  3. The first model said that loans with more months of history default less. That's backwards. Defaulted loans leave the data, so they automatically have fewer months. The feature secretly contained the answer. That's called data leakage. It made the model look better than it really was, with an A U C of zero point eight four instead of zero point seven six.
  4. The model also gave different answers on different runs. The database returned rows in a different order each time, which changed the training split. Sorting the rows fixed it.
  5. And the first real-data version used provincial unemployment to decide where risk is high. It ranked Atlantic Canada riskiest. Checking against real arrears showed Saskatchewan is, despite low unemployment. So the model now uses real arrears for where, and unemployment for when.
  6. The lesson: a result that runs without errors can still be wrong. Each of these was caught by tracing one example back to its source.
